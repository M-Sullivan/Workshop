## Day One: Essential Knowledge for Beginners

### Understanding Wood Finishing

**What is wood finishing?**
Wood finishing is the process of protecting and beautifying wood surfaces. This typically involves removing old finish, repairing damage, and applying new protective coatings.

### Basic Process Overview

Most furniture refinishing projects follow these general steps:

1. **Assessment & Planning**
   - Identify wood type
   - Evaluate current finish
   - Document damage
   - Set realistic goals

2. **Preparation**
   - Clean the piece
   - Remove hardware
   - Set up workspace
   - Gather materials

3. **Stripping (if needed)**
   - Remove old finish using chemical stripper or sanding
   - Clean thoroughly between steps

4. **Repair**
   - Fill cracks, gouges, and holes
   - Tighten joints
   - Replace damaged parts

5. **Sanding**
   - Start with coarse grit, progress to fine
   - Always sand with the grain
   - Remove all dust between grits

6. **Staining (optional)**
   - Apply wood stain to achieve desired color
   - Test on hidden area first

7. **Finishing**
   - Apply protective topcoat (polyurethane, varnish, oil, etc.)
   - Multiple thin coats better than one thick coat
   - Light sanding between coats

8. **Final Assembly**
   - Reinstall hardware
   - Final buffing/polishing

### Essential Safety Information

**Always prioritize safety:**
- Work in well-ventilated area
- Wear appropriate PPE:
  - Safety glasses
  - Dust mask or respirator
  - Gloves (especially with chemical strippers)
  - Hearing protection (when using power tools)
- Read all product labels and safety data sheets
- Keep fire extinguisher nearby (many finishes are flammable)
- Properly dispose of rags soaked in oil-based products (they can spontaneously combust)

### Tools & Materials - Beginner Essentials

**Basic Hand Tools:**
- Screwdrivers (for removing hardware)
- Putty knife or paint scraper
- Sanding block
- Tack cloth (for dust removal)
- Paintbrushes (various sizes)
- Foam brushes
- Stir sticks

**Sandpaper Grits:**
- Coarse (60-80): Heavy material removal
- Medium (100-120): General sanding
- Fine (150-180): Pre-stain sanding
- Very Fine (220-320): Between finish coats

**Chemical Supplies:**
- Chemical stripper (if removing old finish)
- Mineral spirits or paint thinner
- Wood cleaner
- Wood filler/putty
- Stain (if changing color)
- Finish (polyurethane, varnish, oil, wax, etc.)

**Safety Gear:**
- Safety glasses
- Dust masks/respirator
- Chemical-resistant gloves
- Work gloves

### Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Rushing the prep work** - Proper preparation is 80% of the job
2. **Skipping the test area** - Always test stains and finishes on hidden spots
3. **Applying finish too thick** - Multiple thin coats always beat one thick coat
4. **Not sanding between coats** - This creates a smoother final finish
5. **Working in poor conditions** - Temperature, humidity, and ventilation matter
6. **Not removing all hardware** - Take everything off before starting
7. **Sanding against the grain** - This creates scratches that show through finish
8. **Insufficient drying time** - Patience is critical; don't rush between coats

### Key Terminology

- **Grain:** The natural pattern and direction of wood fibers
- **Finish:** The protective coating applied to wood (poly, varnish, lacquer, oil, wax)
- **Stripping:** Removing old finish
- **Staining:** Adding color to wood while still showing grain
- **Sanding:** Smoothing wood with abrasive paper
- **Grit:** The coarseness of sandpaper (lower number = coarser)
- **Tack Cloth:** Sticky cloth for removing fine dust
- **Grain Raising:** When water causes wood fibers to stand up
- **With the Grain:** Sanding/brushing in the direction of wood fibers

### How to Identify Wood Type

Identifying the wood type helps you choose appropriate finishing products and techniques. Here are methods for beginners:

**1. Visual Inspection**
- **Grain Pattern:** Look at the wood grain (may need to check underside or back where finish is less heavy)
  - Tight, straight grain: Often maple, birch, or pine
  - Wide, open grain: Oak, ash, or mahogany
  - Irregular, swirling patterns: Walnut or exotic woods
- **Color:** Natural wood color (check unfinished areas like drawer bottoms, backs, or underside)
  - Light/pale: Maple, birch, pine, ash
  - Reddish-brown: Cherry, mahogany
  - Dark brown: Walnut
  - Yellow/golden: Oak, teak

**2. Check Hidden Areas**
Look at unfinished surfaces where you can see the raw wood:
- Inside drawers
- Underneath the piece
- Back panels
- Inside cabinet compartments
- Bottom of legs

**3. Weight and Hardness**
- **Hardwoods** (oak, maple, cherry, walnut): Heavier, denser, harder to dent
- **Softwoods** (pine, cedar, fir): Lighter weight, easier to dent with fingernail

**4. Grain Texture (Touch Test)**
Run your hand across an unfinished area:
- **Open grain** (feels rough/textured): Oak, ash, mahogany
- **Closed grain** (feels smooth): Maple, cherry, birch

**5. Common Furniture Woods by Era**

*Vintage/Antique (pre-1950s):*
- Oak (very common)
- Walnut
- Mahogany
- Cherry
- Maple

*Mid-Century (1950s-1970s):*
- Teak
- Walnut
- Rosewood
- Oak
- Pine

*Modern Mass-Produced:*
- Oak veneer over plywood/particle board
- Pine
- Birch
- Poplar (usually painted)

**6. Veneer vs Solid Wood**

Many cabinets use veneer (thin layer of nice wood over cheaper wood/particle board):
- Look at edges and corners - do you see layers?
- Check for thin wood peeling or bubbling
- Solid wood shows continuous grain pattern on all sides
- Veneer may have slightly different grain patterns on different surfaces

**7. Ask for Help**
- Take clear photos and post to woodworking forums (r/woodworking, r/finishing)
- Visit local lumber yard or woodworking store with photos
- Bring a photo to a furniture restoration shop
- Ask experienced woodworkers

**8. The "Process of Elimination" Method**
1. Is it veneer or solid wood?
2. Is it hardwood or softwood? (weight/density test)
3. Is the grain open or closed? (texture test)
4. What color is the raw wood?
5. What era does the style suggest?

**For Your Cabinet:**
Document your findings:
- Take photos of any unfinished areas
- Note the weight when you move it
- Check if it's solid wood or veneer
- Look for manufacturer marks or labels (often on back or inside)

### How to Identify the Current Finish

Knowing what finish is already on the wood helps you decide how to remove it and what products to use. Here are tests you can perform:

**Visual Clues:**

**Stain vs Paint:**
- **Stain:** Wood grain is visible through the color
- **Paint:** Wood grain is hidden, opaque coverage
- **Your cabinet:** Since you can see grain, it's stained (or has natural wood with tinted finish)

**Common Finish Types:**

1. **Polyurethane/Varnish** (most common on furniture)
   - Clear, hard, plastic-like coating
   - Can be glossy, semi-gloss, or satin
   - May have yellowed with age
   - Protects well but shows scratches

2. **Lacquer** (common on older/vintage pieces)
   - Very hard, smooth finish
   - Often glossy
   - Brittle, may show crazing (fine cracks)
   - Dries very quickly

3. **Shellac** (common on antiques)
   - Warm, amber tone
   - Not very water-resistant
   - May show white rings from water damage
   - Softer than polyurethane

4. **Oil Finish** (Danish oil, Tung oil, Linseed oil)
   - Soaks into wood, less film on surface
   - Matte or low-sheen appearance
   - Feels closer to raw wood
   - Less protective than poly/varnish

5. **Wax**
   - Very soft, can scratch with fingernail
   - Matte finish
   - Feels slightly waxy to touch
   - Minimal protection

**Simple Tests to Identify Finish:**

**Test 1: Solvent Test (on hidden area)**
Use cotton swabs and test in this order on an inconspicuous spot (inside edge, back corner):

1. **Denatured Alcohol:**
   - Shellac dissolves immediately
   - Lacquer may soften slightly
   - Poly/varnish: no effect

2. **Lacquer Thinner:**
   - Lacquer dissolves quickly
   - Poly/varnish may soften slightly
   - Shellac already dissolved by alcohol

3. **Mineral Spirits:**
   - Oil finishes may darken temporarily
   - Wax may soften
   - Poly/varnish/lacquer: minimal effect

**How to do the test:**
- Dip cotton swab in solvent
- Rub gently on finish for 10-15 seconds
- If finish dissolves/comes off on swab = positive result
- If nothing happens = that's not the finish type

**Test 2: Scratch Test**
In a hidden area, try to scratch with your fingernail:
- **Easily scratches:** Wax or oil finish
- **Resists but can scratch with effort:** Shellac
- **Very hard to scratch:** Polyurethane, varnish, or lacquer

**Test 3: Water Drop Test**
Place a small water drop on hidden area for 5 minutes:
- **White ring/mark:** Likely shellac or lacquer
- **Beads up, no damage:** Polyurethane or varnish
- **Soaks in slightly:** Oil finish or wax

**Age and Condition Clues:**

- **Yellowed, amber tone:** Old varnish or polyurethane
- **Fine crackling (crazing):** Lacquer
- **Cloudy, white spots:** Water damage on shellac/lacquer
- **Sticky in warm weather:** Old varnish breaking down
- **Worn through to wood in high-touch areas:** Thin finish like oil or wax

**For Your Cabinet:**

Based on "darker than natural wood but grain visible":
- Likely **stained wood** (color added)
- PLUS a **topcoat finish** (protection layer)

Common combinations:
- Dark stain + polyurethane (most common modern)
- Dark stain + lacquer (vintage/older pieces)
- Dark stain + shellac (antique pieces)

**Document your findings:**
- Note the sheen (glossy, satin, matte)
- Check for any damage (scratches, water marks, peeling)
- Perform solvent test on hidden spot
- Take close-up photos of the finish
